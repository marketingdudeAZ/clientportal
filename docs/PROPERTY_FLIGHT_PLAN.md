## The problem

The team is reactive, and it is structural rather than cultural. **Today all work is
generated by something going wrong** — a ticket is filed, a score drops, a client
complains. Everything `attention.py` aggregates is a response to an event that
already happened.

Nothing generates work from the calendar. Multifamily is one of the few categories
where the calendar is knowable in advance, so this is a missed structural advantage,
not just a process gap.

## The core mechanic — schedule backwards from the market

A work item is not "SEO refresh sometime this quarter." It is an action with a date
derived from when units actually hit the market, minus every lead time in between.
The unit delivery date is the only fixed point; everything else is computed from it.

    Mar 1   40 units hit the market          <- the only fixed date
    Feb 8   paid flight must be live         (21d ramp)
    Jan 25  creative must be ready           (14d before flight)
    Jan 16  specialist ticket created        (5-7 business day SLA)
    Jan 14  board surfaces the item          <- what the AM sees

Miss Jan 14 and every date after it slips. The units still arrive Mar 1.

The 90-day view an AM opens is this, across every property, sorted by what starts
soonest. "What are we talking about now" becomes "what has to start in the next two
weeks to land on time."

Lead times above are illustrative. Real ones should come from our own delivery data.

## Where the work items come from

Exposure math (already written up in kyle-brain, `wiki/expertise/domain/multifamily-calendar.md`):

    12-month exposure = expirations x (1 - renewal rate)
                        + vacant + notice-to-vacate + month-to-month

Then flight spend lead-time ahead of each vacancy, concentrated on the exposed
floorplans. A flat budget ignores when units open; a flighted one tracks it.

Plus the fixed annual calendar: budget season Aug-Oct (budgets lock Oct/Nov),
renewal pushes Oct-Feb, peak leasing Apr-Aug.

Plus `forecasting.py`, which already emits "recommendations: list of proposed
actions with forecast deltas" — proposed work with a predicted impact attached.

## The router — three lanes, not two

Each work item is routed by task type and track record:

| Lane | What happens | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Auto** | Agent does the work and logs it | Starts nearly empty; earns members over time |
| **Assisted** | Agent drafts, specialist approves or edits | Where most task types start |
| **Specialist** | ClickUp ticket, created early enough to hit the SLA | Backwards-scheduled, not "file it and hope" |

**The Assisted lane is the instrument, not a compromise.** It produces approval rates
per task type — the evidence that says which work is safe to automate. A task type
graduates to Auto when it is approved unedited often enough. We do not decide agents
are ready; we measure it.

This is the same draft-then-approve pattern the Client Brief system already uses.

Every lane writes the same completion record, so the board shows work done rather
than who did it.

`creative_transition.py` is the working template for the entire specialist lane. It
already watches a state change, creates the right ClickUp task, maps a dozen fields
from HubSpot, dedupes so a property can never get two tickets, and degrades
gracefully when a dropdown value does not match. It fires on one trigger today. The
board gives it many.

## Why this matters commercially

"All we do is reporting" is the real problem, and it is what makes our SEO pricing
indefensible against competitors. A report is a dashboard sold at a service price,
and anyone can undercut a dashboard.

A board changes what we sell in three ways at once:

1. **Work actually happens** — items have dates and owners, so delivery stops
   depending on who remembered.
2. **The work becomes visible** — a quarter ends with fourteen completed items
   instead of three PDFs. That is a renewal conversation and a price defense in the
   same artifact.
3. **The cost structure moves** — each task type that graduates to Auto changes the
   margin on every property carrying it, 700 times over.

**Order matters: fix value delivery first, take the savings second.** Automating a
service nobody values just makes an unvalued service cheaper. The specialist savings
are the second-order effect, and they can be taken as margin or redeployed to deep
work agents should not be trusted with yet.

## Three things to get right

1. **Forecast-driven work inherits forecast error.** Our own note says the renewal
   rate is the single most sensitive input and must be stress-tested. Generate the
   first work items from things we *know* — signed expirations, scheduled unit
   deliveries, the fixed calendar — not from modeled projections. Add modeled
   triggers once the known ones prove out.
2. **A client-visible board cuts both ways.** If work slips, the client watches it
   slip in real time. Keep the working board internal; show clients committed and
   completed items, not the messy middle.
3. **Auto without a record is untrustworthy.** Every automated action must log what
   it did, when, and why. The first time a client asks "who changed this," an
   unanswerable question costs the whole model its credibility.

## Build order

1. **One property, one trigger, on paper.** Take a property with known unit
   deliveries in the next 90 days. Generate the work items backwards from that date
   by hand. Show an AM and ask whether it is what they would have done. If no, the
   generator is wrong and no amount of building fixes it. One afternoon, highest
   value step here.
2. **Work items as a table, no UI.** Property, action, why, must-start-by, lane.
   Populated from exposure data plus `forecasting.py` recommendations. Read in Slack.
3. **Wire the specialist lane** to `creative_transition.py`'s ticket-creation
   pattern, scheduled backwards by the SLA. Existing code, no automation risk.
4. **Add Assisted with one task type** — the most repetitive thing specialists do.
   Log every approval and edit; that log is the graduation evidence.
5. **Then build the board UI**, once items, routing, and completion records are real.
   A board over a work generator nobody trusts is just a prettier backlog.
